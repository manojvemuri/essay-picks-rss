Living Fully in the Math World Means Threading the Needle

By Natalie Wolchover

A profile of Fields Medalist Hong Wang that makes the Kakeya problem legible while exploring focus, self-doubt, collaboration, and the cost of exceptional work.

Core idea

Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl’s proof of the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture resolved a central problem connecting harmonic analysis, geometry, partial differential equations, number theory, and combinatorics. Wolchover uses Wang’s Fields Medal profile to show the less glamorous machinery behind a landmark proof: long periods of uncertainty, unusually careful exposition, collaboration, disciplined attention, and the effort required to protect deep work from the obligations that success creates.

Why it stands out

This is not merely an awards profile. It makes a formidable mathematical problem intelligible while examining an uncommon relationship to ambition. Wang is driven less by public recognition than by the desire to understand something with certainty; even after the proof held up, she did not experience the clean triumph outsiders might imagine. The piece is a useful corrective to stories that treat exceptional work as confidence plus talent. Here, progress also depends on doubt, patience, clarity, and the decision to keep working before you know whether you are “good enough.”

Fed up with Big Tech, communities turn to data collectives for control

By Rina Chandran

Communities are pooling language, cultural, agricultural, and genetic data to negotiate how technology companies may use it—and on whose terms.

Core idea

Communities with valuable language, cultural, agricultural, or genetic data are beginning to pool it through cooperatives, trusts, unions, and commons. Instead of allowing technology companies to scrape or appropriate those resources, the groups can set conditions for access, restrict commercial use, demand compensation, and direct data toward local priorities.

Why it stands out

This AI-adjacent piece earns its place because it describes a real institutional alternative rather than repeating abstract arguments about consent. Collective bargaining may work better than one-person-at-a-time permission when the value of a dataset comes from a community. The reporting also avoids presenting cooperatives as a frictionless cure: governance is difficult, scale is expensive, and monetization can undermine the protective mission. The durable strategic insight is that scarce, well-governed data may become both an economic asset and a source of negotiating power.

Iraq’s impossible predicament: why the country is trapped between Iran and the US

By Ghaith Abdul-Ahad

A reported account of how militias, oil dependence, foreign leverage, and regional war have hollowed out Iraqi sovereignty.

Core idea

Iraq’s attempt to balance the United States and Iran has broken down under regional war. Iran-backed militias operate inside the country while the state struggles to control them; attacks on oil infrastructure threaten the revenue on which Iraq depends; and Washington retains enormous leverage because Iraqi oil proceeds pass through the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.

Why it stands out

Abdul-Ahad reports sovereignty not as a legal abstraction but as a practical capability: who can command armed groups, protect infrastructure, retain workers, move money, and speak credibly for the state. The piece shows why a government can possess formal institutions yet remain unable to control the actors and systems on which its survival depends. Its broader lesson is severe and transferable: balancing rival powers works only while neither side forces the intermediary to prove whose authority ultimately matters.

How Nam Cao’s Short Stories Captured a Revolution

By Rory O’Sullivan

A portrait of the Vietnamese writer whose social realism connected private moral failure to colonial poverty, revolution, and political commitment.

Core idea

The Vietnamese writer Nam Cao portrayed moral failure as something produced, or at least intensified, by colonial poverty and social hierarchy. His stories about villagers, teachers, writers, and outcasts link private cruelty and degradation to the economic conditions of French Indochina and the revolutionary politics that preceded independence.

Why it stands out

This is literary criticism that treats fiction as a way of seeing social causation. Nam Cao neither excuses his characters nor isolates their behavior from the systems shaping them. O’Sullivan also captures the tension between art and political commitment: Nam Cao’s humanism helped lead him toward revolutionary activity, while his later work anticipated the narrower demands of official socialist realism. The essay introduces a major writer little translated into English while asking a durable question: how can politically committed art remain morally and psychologically complex?

Don’t turn away from tragedy, but do witness intentionally

By Nena Močnik

A trauma researcher explains how to remain morally engaged with suffering without letting algorithmic exposure produce paralysis, numbness, or burnout.

Core idea

Algorithmic feeds expose people to more graphic suffering than they can meaningfully process or act upon. Močnik argues that witnessing is not equivalent to seeing everything. It requires enough context and emotional space to understand what one has encountered and choose a response. Sustainable witnessing means deliberately limiting sources, time, and scope so moral concern can lead to action rather than exhaustion, numbness, or compulsive reaction.

Why it stands out

This is a rare attention essay that takes moral responsibility seriously. It does not recommend disengagement or treat distress as merely a wellness problem. Instead, it distinguishes exposure from effective solidarity. Choosing one conflict to follow closely, using a few trusted sources, and connecting attention to concrete action can be more ethically serious than consuming an endless stream of suffering. The idea travels beyond news: attention is a finite civic resource, and wasting it through unstructured exposure helps no one.
