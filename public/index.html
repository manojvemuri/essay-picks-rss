Signal then storm

By Stephani Sutherland

A 50-year scientific journey through the neural circuits that turn bodily signals, context, expectation, and attention into the experience of pain.

Core idea

Pain is neither a simple message traveling from damaged tissue to the brain nor a purely mental construction. Sutherland follows neuroscientist Allan Basbaum’s 50-year effort to map the loops through which peripheral nerves, the spinal cord, the brain, context, expectation, and endogenous opioids shape pain before and after it reaches conscious awareness. Basbaum’s unresolved disagreement with his mentor Ronald Melzack remains the essay’s productive center: should treatment begin with the whole lived experience, or with a still-hidden physical driver?

Why it stands out

This is scientific biography doing real explanatory work. It shows a field advancing through failed models, technically demanding experiments, accidental discoveries, placebo effects, and disagreements that do not collapse into a neat synthesis. The article is especially strong on the distinction between nociception—the nervous system’s detection of harmful stimuli—and pain, the subjective experience produced by the larger system. It offers no miracle cure, but it leaves you with a substantially better model of why chronic pain is so difficult to understand and treat.

The hidden cost of Myanmar’s rare earth mines

By Emily Fishbein and Jauman Naw

Deep reporting on how rare-earth supply chains for clean energy, electronics, and weapons finance armed groups and poison Myanmar’s borderlands.

Core idea

Heavy rare earths used in electronics, industrial motors, wind turbines, electric vehicles, and advanced weapons are being extracted from Myanmar’s militarized borderlands under hazardous and opaque conditions. Armed groups tax or protect mines, workers operate without reliable contracts or safety protections, acid-based extraction contaminates land and water, and the material becomes extremely difficult to trace after it enters China’s centralized processing system.

Why it stands out

This is the strongest original reporting in today’s set. It connects individual workers and damaged villages to the strategic language of “critical minerals,” showing how clean-energy and national-security supply chains can externalize their costs into places with weak law and active conflict. The most transferable business lesson is blunt: supply-chain resilience is not achieved merely by identifying an alternative country or supplier. If origin, processing, armed control, labor conditions, and environmental harm disappear inside a blended commodity stream, procurement visibility remains largely fictional.

The Real Problem Underlying India-China Tensions

By Pallavi Aiyar

How colonial memory, the 1962 war, nationalism, and the disappearance of resident journalists cause India and China to misread each other.

Core idea

India and China do not merely disagree about borders, alliances, or relative power; they interpret each other through incompatible historical memories. Aiyar argues that Chinese perceptions of India retain traces of colonial-era subordination and Indian participation in imperial systems, while Indian perceptions of China remain organized around the trauma and perceived betrayal of the 1962 war. Each side then reads contemporary behavior through the old script.

Why it stands out

The essay makes misperception concrete rather than treating it as a diplomatic cliché. It shows how history shapes the emotional register of supposedly pragmatic conversations, and why the same statement can sound like realism to one side and humiliation to the other. Aiyar’s strongest practical point concerns information infrastructure: after years of reciprocal expulsions, India and China have very few resident journalists in each other’s countries and often learn about one another through Western reporting, official messaging, or online nationalism. Restoring sustained, direct observation will not resolve the rivalry, but it can reduce the ignorance through which rivalry hardens.

Did medieval people love their children?

By Samuel Hughes

A compact correction to the persistent myth that high child mortality made medieval parents emotionally detached from their children.

Core idea

The persistent claim that medieval parents were emotionally detached from their children because child mortality was high does not survive the later historical evidence. Hughes summarizes scholarship showing parental sacrifice, joy, grief, long-term financial support, play, education, and family structures far more recognizable than the influential older account associated with Philippe Ariès suggested.

Why it stands out

It is short, but it performs an important intellectual service: it demonstrates how a dramatic thesis about the past can remain culturally influential after specialists have substantially rejected it. The essay’s broader lesson is methodological. When an argument says that an entire class of humans was psychologically alien to us, especially in a way that makes for a striking lecture or story, plausibility should be tested before novelty is rewarded. This is a compact case study in correcting inherited certainty.

A Master of the Traveling Salesperson Problem Finds His Own Path

By Ben Brubaker

A portrait of Shayan Oveis Gharan’s cross-disciplinary approach to algorithms, randomness, graph theory, and long, uncertain mathematical work.

Core idea

Shayan Oveis Gharan, winner of the 2026 IMU Abacus Medal, has advanced algorithms by importing tools from probability, polynomials, graph theory, statistical physics, and other areas that initially look unrelated to the problems at hand. The profile explains his work on the traveling salesperson problem and random sampling while showing how long, uncertain proofs are assembled through detours rather than single flashes of insight.

Why it stands out

Quanta manages two difficult things at once: it makes the technical strategy legible and extracts a useful model of creative work without turning the mathematician into a productivity mascot. Oveis Gharan combines intellectual restlessness with years of patience on individual problems. That balance—continually learning unfamiliar tools while remaining willing to build a proof brick by brick—is a more credible account of breakthrough work than either narrow specialization or shallow interdisciplinarity.
